ACALPHARMA

Global monographic study of the genus Acalypha, with special attention to the species of pharmacological interest.

The Genus Acalypha

Acalypha L. is a very diverse genus of flowering plants in the family Euphorbiaceae, comprising nearly 500 species distributed across tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. The genus is characterised by its herbaceous, shrubby, or sometimes small tree-like growth forms, often with serrated leaves and inconspicuous flowers grouped in catkin-like inflorescences.

Acalypha species appear in most tropical habitats, including rainforests, dry woodlands, and disturbed areas, demonstrating remarkable ecological adaptability. Some species are cultivated as ornamentals for their colourful foliage and unique inflorescences, while others play essential roles in local ecosystems.

Beyond their ecological significance, Acalypha species have received increasing attention for their ethnobotanical and pharmacological potential. Dozens of species have been traditionally used in folk medicine across the world for treating skin conditions, respiratory ailments, and inflammatory disorders, and are now increasingly used in formal pharmacological research.

The ACALPHARMA Project

Taxonomy is often perceived as an old-fashioned, merely descriptive science. It is sometimes seen as no longer necessary, but evidence shows the opposite: taxonomy is now more necessary than never before. We now accumulate data at rates never seen, but the lack of taxonomic expertise and intense data curation makes identification errors widespread.

In 2023, we secured funding from the Spanish Ministry of Science and Innovation to conduct a global-scale study of Acalypha, with special attention to the species in Africa. Our aim is both to improve taxonomic knowledge of Acalypha species and to investigate the origin and evolution of the species known to produce chemical substances of medicinal and pharmacological interest.

Acalypha Taxonomic Information System

The Acalypha Taxonomic Information System is an online platform dedicated to the genus Acalypha via www.acalypha.es. It provides:

  • Rigourous nomenclatural information for ~1,700 published names
  • Data from 50,000+ herbarium specimen records
  • Access to ~25,000 images, including photographs and illustrations
  • Interactive identification keys and a phylogeny viewer
  • Conservation assessments of over 200 species
  • Ethno-pharmacological information of all species with recorded uses
  • Over 1,600 literature references

IPOSTOR

Systematic studies on the genus Ipomoea, the morning glories, with a focus on evolutionary patterns including long-distance dispersal and storage root development

The Genus Ipomoea

Sweet potato is among the ten most important staple foods worldwide and a crop of great potential to help achieve several United Nations Sustainable Development Goals. The crop has high nutritional value, and orange-fleshed varieties, rich in β-carotene (a precursor of vitamin A), have been the focus of recent initiatives promoting health in Sub-Saharan Africa, especially those initiatives aimed at tackling vitamin A deficiency, which affects millions of children worldwide. A crop of American origin, the main sweet potato producers are now countries in South-East Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa. Sweet potato consumption in Europe has also increased steadily in the last decade, and Spain and Portugal are the main sweet potato producers in the continent.

Sweet potato, Ipomoea batatas, is cultivated in all tropical and subtropical regions of the world for its edible storage roots. Ipomoea batatas is the best known species of the genus Ipomoea (family Convolvulaceae) but it is not the only species in the genus that develops storage roots nor the only one consumed by humans. At least 80 wild Ipomoea species have been reported to develop underground storage organs, some of them as big as those in I. batatas or even bigger, and also edible. Some of them are important wild foods and sold in local markets. The study of these wild undomesticated species can provide important information to understand the origin of the sweet potato roots and to identify new sources of genetic diversity for crop improvement.

The IPOSTOR Project

Despite their importance, Ipomoea species with storage roots other than sweet potato are poorly known. For many species we have no accurate information of their geographical distribution, very little genetic data (or no genetic data at all), and no information on the current status of their populations, which makes them virtually invisible to conservation efforts. Importantly, we do not known what determines storage root development in these species nor whether the mechanisms are similar to those in sweet potato. The limited knowledge of most species of Ipomoea with underground storage organs hinders subsequent studies and the promotion of protection measures to tackle population decline and species extinction. It also hinders studies on sweet potato and the identification of new sources of genetic diversity, resistance to pathogens and adaptation to climate change in the crop species. Thus discovering, studying and preserving these species is now a matter of urgency.

This project integrates genomic and transcriptomic analyses with the study of herbarium collections, global biodiversity databases, and extensive fieldwork to study the development and evolution of storage roots in Ipomoea. Specifically, the aims of this project are to investigate whether wild species of Ipomoea with edible roots present traits of interest for sweet potato breeding such as resistance to pathogens or adaptability to changing environmental conditions, and to generate a robust evolutionary framework to study the origin and evolution of storage roots in Ipomoea.
